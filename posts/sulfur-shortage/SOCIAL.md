## LinkedIn

Media: the hero and `images/04-prices-since-2024.png`.

```
Twenty-five years ago, I stepped into my first job out of undergrad, building refinery optimization models for ExxonMobil. Very early on I learned about sulfur, and how important it is to remove it during the refining process.

I honestly haven't given sulfur a second thought in years. That is, until my former boss from ExxonMobil came across Data 4 The People, called me, and recommended I take a look at what's going on with sulfur right now, as it’s a story about refining that few people know, but need to know… if they, you know, eat food.

Sulfur starts out as something refiners need to get rid of and ends up as something fertilizer makers need to buy. It is burned to make sulfuric acid, and that acid is what turns phosphate rock into fertilizer.

U.S. sulfur exports averaged $77 a metric ton in January 2024. In August 2026 the average was $956, a rise of 1,146%. The war closed the Strait of Hormuz, the route for close to half of the sulfur that moves by sea. But the price was already $503 in February 2026, the last full month before the war.

Fertilizer has not kept up. DAP, one of the two main phosphate fertilizers, is up 34%. In January 2024, sulfur was about 5% of the price of a ton of DAP. By August 2026, at export prices, it was close to half. Producers have responded by making less.

Now sulfur prices are easing. But the sulfur saga seems far from over. In fact, it could be a canary in the coal mine for the risks that are building in the broader global energy markets.

The full report, with seven charts and a free price tracker, is linked in the first comment.
```
