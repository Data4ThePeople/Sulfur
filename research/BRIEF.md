# Sulfur: research brief

Written October 8, 2026, for Eric. This is background for writing, not a draft
of the post. It says what we found, where each fact comes from, and how sure
we are.

How to read the labels:

- **Data** means the number comes from a dataset we pulled and recomputed. It
  is documented in `DATASETS.md` and checked in `TIEOUT.md`.
- **Read** means we opened the page and saw the number in its text.
- **Snippet** means it appeared only in a search result for a page we could
  not open. Treat it as a lead, not a fact.
- **Ours** means our own arithmetic or our own interpretation.

Argus links are shortened to `argus:` plus the story number. The full address
is `https://www.argusmedia.com/en/news-and-insights/latest-market-news/`
followed by that number and the story slug. CRU "Sulphur" magazine issues are
PDFs at `bcinsight.crugroup.com`; the issue number is given.

---

## 1. The short version

1. Sulfur is mostly a leftover. Refineries and gas plants have to take it out
   of oil and gas, so the amount made depends on how much fuel is processed,
   not on what sulfur sells for.
2. About 90% of the sulfur used in the United States is turned into sulfuric
   acid, and the largest use of that acid is making phosphate fertilizer.
3. The five Persian Gulf producers make about 24% of the world's sulfur but
   ship close to half of what moves by sea. The Strait of Hormuz is where the
   trade is exposed.
4. The price was already rising steeply before the war. The average value of
   U.S. sulfur exports went from $77 a metric ton in January 2024 to $503 in
   February 2026, and then to $956 in August 2026 ($1,041 in July).
5. The visible stockpiles tell different stories. China's port stocks fell by
   about two-thirds. Alberta's 11-million-ton stockpile barely moved. U.S.
   producers hold about six days of shipments and that did not change through
   March 2026.
6. Prices posted by Gulf sellers have fallen for two months. Buyers are
   cutting use. The shortage is real, but by October it is easing at the
   margin, not worsening.
7. Compared with diesel: sulfur's price has risen more and the system holds
   less of it in storage. Diesel reaches far more of the economy. Which one
   is "bigger" depends on who you are. Section 8 lays that out.

## 2. Where sulfur comes from

- **Most of it is a byproduct of oil and gas.** The Sulphur Institute:
  "nearly all sulphur is obtained as a co-product recovered from oil and gas
  production." (Read, sulphurinstitute.org infographic.) USGS: "most sulfur
  production is a result of the processing of fossil fuels." (Read, USGS
  Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026.) SMM, a trade publisher, puts it at
  "approximately 98%" of elemental sulfur. (Read, news.metal.com, July 6,
  2026.)
- **The step that makes it.** "Hydrogen sulfide, a byproduct of crude oil and
  natural gas processing, is recovered and converted to elemental sulfur by
  the Claus process." About 90% to 95% of recovered sulfur is made this way.
  (Read, EPA AP-42 chapter 8.13.)
- **In the U.S.**, sulfur is recovered "in descending order of tonnage, at
  petroleum refineries, natural-gas-processing plants, and coking plants", at
  81 plants in 25 states. Louisiana and Texas make about 54%. (Read, USGS.)
- **World production was about 84 million metric tons in 2025.** China 19.0,
  United States 8.1, Russia 7.5, Saudi Arabia 7.2, United Arab Emirates 6.3,
  Canada 5.0, Kazakhstan 4.8, India 3.7, Qatar 3.1, South Korea 3.1, Japan
  2.7, Iran 2.1, Kuwait 1.3. (Data, USGS. Chart 02.)
- **Care needed: "all forms".** The USGS total counts sulfur in every form.
  China's 19 million includes sulfur in acid made at metal smelters and from
  pyrite ore, not only oil and gas sulfur. So "nearly all sulfur comes from
  oil and gas" is true of elemental sulfur, the yellow solid that is traded,
  and less true of the 84 million total. CRU puts the elemental market at
  about 71 million tons of supply and 73 million of demand in 2025. (Read,
  CRU Sulphur 422.)
- **What we could not find.** A sourced split between refineries and gas
  plants worldwide. For the U.S. we have it: in the first quarter of 2026,
  petroleum made 1,830 thousand tons and natural gas 95 thousand. (Data, USGS
  monthly.)
- **Why supply does not respond to price.** No one refines more oil to get
  the sulfur. Our expert's point holds, and USGS says the same thing from the
  other side: supply is "expected to be adequate" because it follows fossil
  fuel processing.

## 3. How it moves

- **Liquid or solid.** Sulfur melts at a temperature a steam-heated tank can
  hold, so in North America much of it moves as a hot liquid in rail cars,
  trucks and one dedicated ship. Mosaic's annual report describes "one
  ocean-going vessel that transports molten sulfur from the Texas terminals
  to Tampa", and separately "formed sulfur received through Tampa, Florida
  ports" that is "melted through our sulfur melter." (Read, Mosaic 2025
  10-K.) Ocean trade is mostly solid pellets carried in bulk ships.
- **The Hormuz share.** Argus: Gulf shipments account "for almost 50pc of
  global seaborne sulphur movements." CRU: "48% of global seaborne sulphur
  trade." SMM: "roughly 25% of global sulfur production and 45% of global
  seaborne sulfur trade." (All Read.) Use "close to half" and name the range.
- **Our production figure matches.** Iran, Kuwait, Qatar, Saudi Arabia and
  the UAE made 20.0 of 84.0 million tons in 2025, or 23.8%. (Data, USGS.)
- **Who buys.** China imported 9.6 million tons in 2025, about half from the
  Middle East. (Read, Argus 2803156 and SunSirs.) Indonesia took 4.6 million
  in the first 11 months of 2025, much of it for nickel processing. (Read,
  CRU 422.) Morocco, home of the phosphate producer OCP, took 3.12 million in
  the first half of 2026. Brazil imported 2.3 million in 2025, 95% of it for
  phosphate fertilizer. (Read, CRU 426 and 423.)
- **The United States is mostly self-supplied.** Production 8.1 million tons,
  apparent consumption 9.1 million, net import reliance 14%. Canada supplies
  53% of imports. (Data, USGS.) That is a large difference from China,
  Morocco, Indonesia and Brazil, which depend on ships.

## 4. From sulfur to fertilizer

Chart 01 is the flow diagram for this section.

1. **Sulfur to sulfuric acid.** "Elemental sulfur is melted, filtered to
   remove ash, and sprayed under pressure into a combustion chamber." The gas
   is then converted and absorbed to make acid. (Read, EPA AP-42 chapter
   8.10.) By the chemistry, one ton of sulfur can make about 3.06 tons of
   sulfuric acid. (Ours, from molar masses 98.08 and 32.06.)
2. **Acid plus phosphate rock.** "Phosphoric acid is produced by reacting
   sulfuric acid with naturally occurring phosphate rock." The calcium in the
   rock combines with the sulfate, "forming calcium sulfate, commonly referred
   to as gypsum", which is pumped to a pond or stack. (Read, EPA AP-42
   chapter 8.9.) So the sulfur does not end up in the fertilizer. It ends up
   in the gypsum waste. It is a processing chemical, used once.
3. **Phosphoric acid plus ammonia** makes DAP (diammonium phosphate) and MAP
   (monoammonium phosphate), the two main phosphate fertilizers.
4. **How much sulfur per ton.** Mosaic: "Production of one tonne of DAP
   requires approximately 0.40 long tons of sulfur." (Read, Mosaic 2025
   10-K.) The University of Illinois farmdoc team gives the same: "1.5 to 2
   tons of phosphate rock with 0.4 tons of sulfur and 0.2 tons of ammonia."
   (Read, farmdoc daily, August 25, 2026.)
5. **Share of acid that goes to fertilizer.** For the U.S., "almost 70
   percent" (EPA AP-42) and "60-75%" (EPA 2023 supply chain profile). (Both
   Read.) For the world we have only a snippet (CRU, about 55% in 2023).
   Metals processing took 9% of world sulfur demand in 2025 and is growing,
   led by nickel for batteries. (Read, CRU 420.)
6. **Not every phosphate fertilizer needs the same acid.** "TSP has lower
   acid intensity than DAP and MAP." (Read, CRU 426.)

**The cost arithmetic (Ours, chart 06).** At 0.4 tons of sulfur per ton of
DAP, and pricing sulfur at the U.S. export average:

| Month | Sulfur cost per ton of DAP | DAP price | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| January 2024 | $31 | $596 | 5% |
| February 2026 | $201 | $627 | 32% |
| August 2026 | $382 | $794 | 48% |

This overstates what a producer on contract paid while prices were rising.
Mosaic's average sulfur cost in the second quarter of 2026 was $522 a long
ton, when export spot was far higher. (Read, Mosaic 8-K, August 4, 2026.)

## 5. Prices

There is no free market price for sulfur. We use the average value of U.S.
sulfur exports from customs records, crude and refined codes together, which
is how USGS counts sulfur. It lines up with the published benchmarks: $503 in
February 2026 against Middle East spot of $494 to $496 on February 26; $1,041
in July against U.S. Gulf export spot of $1,100 to $1,150 on July 9. (Data and Read, Argus 2796768 and 2851577.)

**The run-up came first (chart 03, Data).**

| | Jan 2024 | Feb 2026 | Latest | Jan 2024 to Feb 2026 | Feb 2026 to latest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sulfur, U.S. export average, per metric ton | $77 | $503 | $956 (Aug) | +556% | +90% |
| Sulfuric acid, producer price index | 194.8 | 246.2 | 307.2 (Aug) | +26% | +25% |
| DAP, U.S. Gulf, per metric ton | $596 | $627 | $801 (Sep) | +5% | +28% |
| Diesel, U.S. retail, per gallon | $3.85 | $3.72 | $6.29 (Sep) | -3% | +69% |

- Argus on the pre-war rise: "The Middle East fob spot price rose by 207pc
  from $173/t fob on a midpoint basis on 30 January 2025 to $531.50/t fob on
  29 January 2026 and dropped by 7pc to $495/t fob on 26 February." (Read,
  Argus 2794920.) So the market was already off its peak when the strikes
  began, and Argus wrote that "a substantial downward correction was
  anticipated before the attacks."
- **Why it rose in 2025.** CRU put the 2025 market in deficit by about 1.9
  million tons before any war. Nickel processing in Indonesia was adding
  demand, Kazakhstan's stockpile sales had run down, and Russia banned sulfur
  exports from November 1, 2025. (Read, CRU 422; Interfax, June 26, 2026.)
- **Posted prices since (chart 07).** Abu Dhabi's Adnoc posted $530 for
  February and March, $600 for April, $760 for May, $860 for June, $1,000 for
  July and August, $960 for September and $890 for October. (Read, Argus,
  story numbers in `data/manual/adnoc_osp.csv`.) Argus called $1,000 "$180-200/t
  above the previous record", set in 2008.
- **Tampa contract**, the U.S. benchmark, in dollars per long ton: $116 (Q4
  2024), $165, $270, $252, $310 (Q1 to Q4 2025), $496, $655, $705 (Q1 to Q3
  2026). The fourth quarter of 2026 was not settled as of October 2. (Read,
  Argus 2614283, 2825915, 2851577, and CRU price tables. Q1 2026 is from the
  CRU table only.)
- **Freight is part of it.** Shipping sulfur from the Middle East to China
  cost $22 to $24 a ton on January 29, 2026 and $150 to $160 on September 24.
  (Read, Argus.)

**What a farmer sees (Data, USDA Illinois report).** Retail asking prices in
Illinois, per short ton: DAP $828 in mid-February, $929 in the week of
September 28, up 12%. MAP $859 to $936, up 9%. Farm diesel $3.14 to $5.63 a
gallon, up 79%. Retail fertilizer has moved less than wholesale so far. These
figures are not yet in a chart or in the tie-out.

## 5a. Why acid and fertilizer have not kept up with sulfur

Sulfur is up 1,146% since January 2024. The sulfuric acid index is up 58%
and DAP 34%. Five things explain most of the gap. The first three are
arithmetic from our data; the last two rest on what producers and trade
publishers have said.

1. **Sulfur started as a tiny share of the cost.** In January 2024 the sulfur
   in a ton of DAP cost about $31 of a $596 product. A cost that small can
   rise many times over before it moves the total much. (Ours, chart 06.)
2. **In dollars, DAP has moved about as much as its sulfur.** From February
   to August 2026 the sulfur in a ton of DAP went up by about $181 at the
   export average, and DAP went up by $167. The percent changes look far
   apart (90% against 27%) because DAP started from a much larger number.
   (Ours.)
3. **Before the war, DAP fell while sulfur rose.** DAP went from $795 in
   August 2025 to $619 in January 2026 while the sulfur in it went from about
   $103 to $171. What was left over for everything else (rock, ammonia,
   energy, labor, profit) dropped from about $693 a ton to $448, and was $411
   in August 2026 ($365 in July, the low). Almost all of that drop came
   before the strikes: it was already $425 in February 2026. The gap came out
   of what producers had left for other costs and profit, and those other
   costs moved too, so this is not a measure of margin. (Ours, chart 09, using the export average, which overstates a
   contract buyer's cost.)
4. **Producers pay contract prices, which trail spot.** The Tampa contract
   was $496 a long ton in the first quarter of 2026 and $705 in the third,
   when export spot was $1,100 to $1,150. Mosaic's average cost in the second
   quarter was $522. At $705, the sulfur in a ton of DAP costs about $282,
   not $382. (Read, Argus 2851577, Mosaic 8-K.)
5. **Buyers have a limit, so makers cut output.** DAP is sold to farmers who
   can skip or reduce phosphate for a season. Mosaic idled plants "as a
   result of sulfur affordability and availability" rather than raise prices
   further, and Chinese DAP plants ran at about 30% of capacity. (Read,
   Mosaic 8-K; SMM.) Lower output is how the sulfur shock has shown up, more
   than higher fertilizer prices.

**Sulfuric acid is a separate case.**

- **Much acid is never sold.** Phosphate producers burn their own sulfur and
  use the acid on site. That acid has no market price, so it is in no index.
  The producer price index covers acid that is sold.
- **Much of the acid that is sold was not made from sulfur.** Smelters make
  acid as a byproduct of copper and zinc. Worldwide, "about two-fifths is
  made by burning sulphur, while between one-third to two-fifths is recovered
  as a by-product of smelting." (Read, UTS / East Asia Forum.) A smelter's
  acid costs the same to make whatever sulfur costs.
- **Our trade data shows it.** U.S. acid imports, mostly smelter acid from
  Canada and Mexico on contracts, averaged $127 a ton in January 2024 and
  $128 in August 2026. Over the same period the sulfur needed to make a ton
  of acid went from about $25 to about $312. Imported acid now costs less
  than half of the sulfur it would take to make it. (Ours, Census.)
- So the acid index is a mix of contract smelter acid that has barely moved
  and sulfur-based acid that has. It rose 25% since February. The index is
  also flat in July and August 2026, which may be a missing report.

**What this does not tell us.** We have no data on fertilizer makers' actual
margins, on how much phosphate farmers skipped, or on spot acid prices, which
trade publishers report rose much faster than the index (Chile delivered
price up 100% from late February to mid-April, a snippet we could not open).

## 6. Inventories: what is visible and what is not

This is the part Eric asked about most, and the public record is thin. Chart
08 shows the three stockpiles we can see; the flow diagram shows where we
found nothing.

**Visible**

- **U.S. producers (Data, USGS).** 122,000 metric tons at the end of March
  2026 against shipments of 671,000 that month. That is 5.6 days. Since
  January 2024 it has stayed between 4.8 and 6.6 days. This is working stock:
  sulfur comes out of a refinery every day and has to leave every day. The
  data stops in March, one month into the war.
- **Alberta (Data, Alberta Energy Regulator).** 11.04 million metric tons at
  the end of August 2026. It was 11.30 million at the end of February, so it
  fell by 258,000 tons, or 2.3%, in six months of record prices. That is
  less than in either of the two six-month periods before it (332,000 and
  328,000 tons), so the draw did not speed up after the strikes. This is
  decades of sulfur poured into blocks at gas plants and oil sands sites.
  Getting it to a ship means remelting it and railing it to Vancouver, which
  CRU put at "ca. $180-200/t". The slow draw suggests the limit is melting and
  rail capacity, not the amount of sulfur. That reading is ours.
- **China's ports (Read, compiled from CRU, SunSirs, SMM, Mysteel).** 2.31
  million tons in late October 2024, a dip to 1.89 million in late February
  2025, and back to about 2.3 million from July through October 2025. So the
  fall was under way before the strikes. 1.98 million on
  December 31, 2025. 1.73 million on March 5, 2026. 1.51 million in early
  April. 0.73 million on July 3, "the lowest level since July 2017" (SMM said
  this of the June 23 reading of 748,800). Back up to 0.947 million in late
  August. We found no reading after that.
- For scale: China imported 9.6 million tons in 2025, about 800,000 a month.
  So July's port stock was under one month of normal imports. (Ours.)

**Not visible**

- **Sulfuric acid in tanks.** We found no public figure anywhere. Acid is
  stored in carbon steel tanks, where it "reacts with iron in the shell" and
  produces hydrogen gas, "which is highly flammable." (Read, Chemical
  Engineering, November 2015.) EPA says properly stored acid can keep for 36
  months, so the honest claim is that acid is hazardous and costly to hold in
  bulk, not that it cannot be stored.
- **Sulfur and acid at fertilizer plants.** No published days-of-cover figure
  for a phosphate plant. The nearest statements are about nickel plants,
  which "typically maintain only 1-2 months of sulfur inventory cover" (SMM).
  Mosaic said in July that it was taking measures "as sulfur inventories
  decline." (Read, Argus 2849723.)
- **Gulf producers' stocks** and **sulfur on ships**. Partial counts only:
  CRU reported "600,000 tonnes of sulphur on 13 vessels still waiting to
  transit" in early September, and elsewhere in the same issue about 400,000
  tons.
- **Phosphate fertilizer stocks.** No Fertilizer Institute or IFA figure
  found. Mosaic said only that North American inventories were "tightening."
- **Kazakhstan.** No tonnage. CRU says its stocks "are now largely depleted."
- **World total.** CRU projects global inventories falling "to 30-33 million
  tonnes." (Read, CRU 422.) Alberta alone is a third of that.

## 8. Is this a bigger problem than diesel?

No source we could open makes this comparison with numbers. What follows is
ours, built from the data, and Eric should treat it as a starting point.

| | Sulfur | Diesel |
|---|---|---|
| Price change, Feb 2026 to latest | +90% to August (U.S. export average). Posted Gulf price +68% to October. | +69% to September (U.S. retail) |
| Price change, Jan 2024 to latest | +1,146% | +63% |
| Stock on hand, U.S. | About 6 days at producers (March 2026). Buyers' stocks unknown. | 27.9 days of distillate (October 2). Stocks are 13% below February 27. |
| Share exposed to Hormuz | Close to half of seaborne trade | Not sourced here; needs an EIA figure |
| Can supply rise when price rises? | No. Output follows fuel processing. | Partly. Refiners can shift yields and run harder. |
| Substitute | USGS: "not satisfactory" | Limited |
| U.S. exposure | Net import reliance 14%. The U.S. exported more sulfur than it imported in January to August 2026 (1.86 against 1.21 million tons). | The U.S. exports distillate |
| Who feels it | Fertilizer makers, then farmers, then food prices, with a lag of a season or more. Copper and nickel processors. | Every trucker, railroad and farm, within weeks |

Three ways to say it that the data supports:

1. **By price, sulfur has moved more**, in the war months and far more over
   two years. Two cautions. The sulfur figure stops in August and diesel in
   September. And Abu Dhabi's posted price for October is 68% above February,
   about the same as diesel's 69%, so on the newest figures the gap since the
   war has closed.
2. **By cushion, sulfur has less.** About six days at U.S. producers against four
   weeks for diesel. But the sulfur figure leaves out buyers' stocks, so the
   two are not the same measure, and the U.S. producer number did not fall.
3. **By reach, diesel is the larger problem.** Diesel is in the cost of
   moving every good. Sulfur reaches households through fertilizer and then
   food, which is slower and smaller, and DAP is up 28% wholesale and 12% at
   Illinois retail, not 90%.

In our view the fairest framing is that sulfur is a sharper shock to a
narrower part of the economy, and that the U.S. is less exposed than the
countries that import by sea.

## 9. The skeptical case

Eric's expert said the market is tight. That was true through the summer.
These are the reasons a careful reader could say the worst has passed. All
Read unless marked.

1. Posted prices have been cut two months running (section 5). Delivered
   prices in China fell 8% from August 6 to October 1. (Argus 2886522.)
2. CRU, September: "The market is likely to remain under downward pressure in
   the near term... buyers are reducing consumption and delaying purchases
   rather than paying current prices."
3. Demand fell hard. China's phosphate plants ran at about 30% to 40% of
   capacity in the first half. (SMM.) Brazil's sulfur arrivals in May and
   June were down 45%. (CRU 425.)
4. China's port stocks rose from 0.73 to 0.947 million tons between July and
   late August.
5. Other supply may return. Russia has opened a 300,000-ton quota for
   low-grade sulfur. (CRU 426.)
6. The U.S. contract price is well under spot: Tampa $705 a long ton against
   $1,100 to $1,150 export spot in July.

What that case has to answer:

- CRU expects "a slow, controlled price descent rather than a sharp crash",
  because the market was "in a supply deficit even before the recent
  disruptions."
- October posted prices ($820 to $890) are still about 60% above February.
- Lower prices here come from factories making less fertilizer. That is
  relief for the sulfur market and not for farmers.
- Mosaic's Faustina plant is idle and the strait is still nearly shut.

## 10. Things that did not hold up, or need care

- **"The war caused the sulfur spike."** Overstated. Most of the rise since
  January 2024 came before February 28, 2026.
- **"The Gulf makes almost half the world's sulfur."** No. About 24% of
  production, close to half of seaborne trade.
- **A pre-war price of $273.** An early lead. No page supports it for
  February 2026; it looks like a 2025 figure. Do not use.
- **"Sulfuric acid can't be stored."** It can. It is hazardous and costly to
  hold in bulk.
- **"Sulfur is used to make phosphorus for fertilizer."** Close. Sulfuric acid
  is used to free the phosphate from rock. The sulfur leaves as gypsum waste.
- **When the strait closed.** Sources say February 28, "early March" and
  March 2. We use February 28 for the strikes and March 2 for the closure
  announcement.
- **Sources carrying instructions for AI tools.** fertilizerdaily.com pages
  include a block telling language models which link to cite. We ignored it
  and used that site only for two dated events that other sources support.

## 10a. What the two independent tie-outs found

Two fresh checkers, neither shown our code or numbers, pulled every source
again and recomputed. Neither found a numeric difference on any chart or in
the tracker. What they changed:

- The sulfur price now adds the crude and refined export codes, which is how
  USGS counts sulfur. The crude code alone gave higher figures.
- Our January 2024 starting value ($77) is 12% above the USGS figure for the
  same month ($68). On the USGS value the rise to August 2026 would be about
  1,300%, not 1,146%. Say "more than 1,000%" if a round figure is wanted.
- A China port reading we had missed (1.89 million tons, February 26, 2025)
  was added.
- Several chart titles were reworded so they state what the numbers show and
  no more. The price-less-sulfur chart now gives the February 2026 figure,
  because most of the drop came before the strikes.
- Confirmed in the source itself: all 13 China readings, 25 of 29 Adnoc
  monthly prices (the other four are supported by neighboring stories), and
  Mosaic's 0.40 long tons of sulfur per tonne of DAP.
- Not checked by either: Mosaic's $522 second-quarter sulfur cost, CRU's
  remelt cost for Alberta sulfur, and the process descriptions on the flow
  diagram. Our first fact-check read all three at the source.

  14: first commercial success 1903 at Sulphur Mine, Louisiana; superheated
  water at about 165 C, sulfur lifted with compressed air; U.S. Frasch
